The Second Impression

How to prepare for IB Mathematics Analysis and Approaches Higher Level, and how to know it is the right choice.

A reading for families choosing Higher Level.

The message usually arrives a few months into the first year. A parent, apologetic, a little bewildered. Their child has always been excellent at mathematics. Top of the class since primary school, the one the others ask for help. Something has gone wrong now, and nobody can say exactly what.

I ask which topic they are on. Often it is vectors. Sometimes proof. Sometimes complex numbers, which arrived in the second month and never quite settled.

Then I ask the question that matters, which is when it started. The answer is almost always the same. It did not start. It simply never began well.

What the course actually asks

Analysis and Approaches at Higher Level has a reputation and it has earned it. Teachers say so openly in the first week, sometimes as a warning and sometimes as a boast. A class that begins the year with ten students often ends it with six. That is not a verdict on the four who left. It is a fact about the course, and it is worth knowing before you choose it.

The difficulty is not that the mathematics is heavier. It is that the course asks for something school has not asked for before.

Proof arrives with nothing behind it. No IGCSE or MYP topic prepares a student to write an argument rather than an answer, and marks go missing for a conclusion that was only implied. Complex numbers hand you an entirely new number system, so there is nothing from earlier study to reason from. Vectors arrives late, sits inside geometry and trigonometry, and chains four or five techniques into a single question. Ask Higher Level graduates which topic shook them and vectors is the answer you hear most.

These three have something in common that the syllabus does not mention. None of them settles on a first reading. They are the kind of ideas that need to be met, put down, and met again. The syllabus grants exactly one meeting, at speed, with an examination waiting.

The strongest students, first

Here is the part that surprises parents. The students who feel it first are often the strongest ones.

A student who has always found mathematics easy has never had to develop a method for not understanding. They have never needed one. Their whole experience of the subject is that they read a question, recognise it, and know what to do. When that stops working, they have no practice at the state of not knowing, and so they read it as a verdict on themselves rather than as a description of the material.

Students who have had to work for their marks arrive with something the top student often lacks. A tolerance for confusion. A habit of staying in a problem that has not opened yet. It is an unglamorous skill and it turns out to be the one Higher Level asks for most.

Ten minutes

Almost everything a student comes to believe about a mathematical idea is decided in the first ten minutes they spend with it.

That is not how it ought to work. It is how it does. The first meeting with proof, or with vectors, happens in a classroom in the middle of a crowded term, with three other subjects due and an examination in view, and it is over before it has properly begun. Whatever impression forms in those ten minutes tends to harden into something the student then carries for years.

Students will tell you years later that they were never good at vectors. What they mean is that they met vectors on a Tuesday in March, when three other subjects were also due.

This is why the usual remedy does not work as well as it should. More time on a topic, after the impression has already formed, is spent arguing with a belief rather than teaching an idea. The student is not only learning vectors. They are learning vectors while quietly convinced they are the sort of person who cannot do vectors.

The second impression

The remedy is not more time. It is an earlier meeting.

When a student meets vectors for the first time in a quiet room in July, with nothing at stake, no marks attached and no examination in view, something changes about the second meeting. The classroom in March is no longer where the belief gets formed. It is where it gets confirmed. The student is not deciding whether they can do this. They decided that in July, and they decided yes.

That is what I mean by a second impression, and it is where this essay takes its name. A first impression is formed under pressure, in a hurry, with everything at stake. A second impression is something else entirely. You are no longer forming a belief. You are refining one.

It is a modest intervention. It does not make anyone cleverer. All it does is move the first meeting to a place where the student can afford to find it difficult.

Before, or after

Almost all help in mathematics arrives after something has gone wrong. That is the shape of the whole industry. A mark drops, a parent worries, a tutor is found. The tutor then has to repair one topic while the class has already moved on to the next, so the student is doing two things at once, catching up and keeping up, in the term when there is least room for either.

It works, often. It is also the most expensive and least pleasant way to arrive at the same place.

This is the difference between tutoring and enrichment, and the words deserve more precision than they usually get. Tutoring follows the school. Its agenda is set by what was covered last week and what is due on Friday. Enrichment runs ahead of the school. Its agenda is set by what is coming and by what is known to cause trouble when it comes.

Tutoring is a response. Enrichment is a preparation. A student may well need both, and there is no quarrel here with good tutoring, which repairs real damage and does it kindly. The observation is only about order. Almost everyone buys the response and almost nobody buys the preparation, and the preparation is cheaper, calmer and more effective.

When to begin

Before the topics arrive, which is less demanding than it sounds. Being ahead of the syllabus does not mean being ahead of day one.

Proof does come early. Complex numbers usually land around the middle of the first year, and vectors late in the first year or into the second. A student who begins two months into the course is still comfortably ahead of the material that does the damage. What matters is the gap between the first meeting and the classroom, not how early in the calendar it happens.

The practical answer for most families is the holiday before Higher Level begins, or the first weeks of the course itself.

Whether to take it at all

There is another question underneath all of this, and parents tend to ask it more quietly. Should my child be taking Higher Level in the first place?

Sometimes the answer is no, and it should be said plainly rather than softened. Standard Level is not a lesser subject and it is not a retreat. For a student whose real work lies elsewhere, it is the better choice, and it leaves room for that work to happen.

What I would not do is decide in the middle of a hard term. A student who moves down three weeks after meeting vectors badly is not making a decision about their ability. They are making a decision about an impression, formed in ten minutes, on a bad Tuesday.

Give them the second impression first, and then decide. Some will stay and flourish, and will have chosen Higher Level rather than merely survived it. Some will move to Standard Level with a clear head instead of a bruised one, which is a much better way to arrive anywhere.

* * *

I cannot give a student more ability. Nobody can, and anyone promising it should be treated with suspicion. What can be given is a better first ten minutes with the ideas that decide how the next two years will feel.

It turns out that is most of what was missing.

Lerato Molisana
