The Portrait

A mark tells you where you finished. It says nothing about how you got there.

A written account of how you actually think about mathematics, made over two short sittings.

Two students answer the same question correctly. One recognised the shape of it immediately and reached for a method they have used a hundred times. The other did not recognise it at all, and built something from an idea they understood properly. The mark cannot tell them apart. Almost everything worth knowing about their mathematics is in the difference.

School has measured you often. Tests, examinations, class ranks, predicted grades. All of them record what happened at the end. Almost none of them describe what you did along the way, which is the part that decides what you should study next and how it should be taught to you.

The Portrait is the description. Over two sittings you work through questions built to show how you read, how you set your reasoning down, and what you do when nothing looks familiar. There is no mark at the end of it, and no percentile. What you receive is written.

Read

How you take mathematics in. Whether the notation is fluent or effortful, whether you can tell the difference between understanding something and recognising it, and what you do with a definition the first time you meet one.

Fluency · Calibration · Reading

Write

How you set reasoning down. Whether a reader can follow your working without you in the room, and whether what you have written is an argument or a set of steps that happen to end in the right place.

Expression · Argument

Think

What happens when nothing is familiar. How long you stay with a problem that has not opened yet, whether you can work from a definition alone, and how you behave in the part of mathematics nobody has taught you.

Independence · Abstraction

Ninety minutes cannot show me everything, and the report says so where it matters. Persistence, and the kind of idea that arrives on the fourth day of thinking about something, do not appear in a single sitting. I would rather tell you what the Portrait could not see than let you assume it saw all of it.

What it does see, it describes plainly. It writes about habits rather than about you, because a habit is a thing you can change and a verdict is not. Nobody is told what kind of student they are.
